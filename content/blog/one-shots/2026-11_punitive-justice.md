Since so many people seem to endorse punitive justice as a principle, you'd be tempted to assume there must be some form of legitimate logic behind it, but is that true? Why must every single wrongdoing be repaid in kind? Why must rehabilitation be measured in blood, deprivation, and humiliation rather than accountability, mending, and growth?

Of course, I can understand and empathize with the emotional urge to strike back and get even, but I hardly see how that makes for a receivable argument.

If such a law is indeed etched in the transcendent human code of morality, why must its rationale remain ineffable and elusive?

Ironically, if you think about it long enough, you will realize the actual answer is quite straightforward: punitive justice is irrational and unethical. Punishment can be justified in specific cases but only as a lesser evil, never as an end in itself.

This is probably a bold claim to many, but I can back it up. First, we need to consider what it means to justify a moral prescription.

## Grounding ethics

A claim is justified if it logically follows from a given premise. Accordingly, we first need to identify our ethical premise to figure out whether punitive justice can be justified.

As it happens, this is a surprisingly difficult thing to do. To avoid a protracted epistemological digression, I'll simply mention that it's impossible to make a foolproof philosophical justification for any given claim^[If you'd like to dig further, I've covered this topic in the past in [my video on the Münchhausen trilemma](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=R4Kbr-ixZns).]. The only way to achieve partial knowledge is filling in at least a few gaps with assumptions, which begs the question: which assumptions should we base our morality on?

In the absence of a unanimous and unarguable foundation for ethics, we will have to operate on a humbler and more practical level. Human moral intuitions are the basis of all ethical frameworks, and despite some inter-individual variations, the bulk of their central principles are largely universal.

My core moral assumption is [universal agency](/about/universal-agency/). Yours may be slightly different. Regardless, by following their logical ramifications, there's much to bet they will both quickly converge towards a crucial consensus: maximizing human^[Depending on the individual, this may expand to include animals and other living organisms to various extents.] flourishing and, consequently, minimizing harm.

Why are murder, theft, deceit, cruelty, infidelity, and the like largely considered immoral? Because they cause material, psychological, emotional, or physical harm. Or at least, they have the potential to do so. Why are healing, charity, honesty, empathy, loyalty, and the like largely considered virtuous? Because they preserve or improve well-being, either directly or by preventing harm and alleviating suffering.

To get back to our original topic, if you do agree with harm reduction as a fundamental ethical principle, then it logically follows that you cannot sanction punitive justice. That's the only requirement for my argument.

## Retribution is not harm reduction

> Deserve's got nothin' to do with it.
>
> – *Unforgiven* (1992)

Barring far-fetched and mind-boggling technological breakthroughs in time travel, there's no changing the past. What's done is done and cannot be undone.

Seeing as we can only experience and influence the present and the future, the only reasonable function for the past is to become a harvestable repository of information to pave the ideal path forward as best we can thereafter.

As the saying goes, it's no use crying over spilt milk. However, there is mileage to be found in clearing out the broken glass and mopping up the mess, as well as in troubleshooting and optimizing the prevention of future potential dairy spillage.

Allowing ourselves to remain beholden to the past is falling prey to the [sunk cost fallacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunk_cost). It will only keep us stuck and hold back what good may yet be revealed and accomplished. This is not just a personal pet theory but an established tenet from economics. According to the bygones principle, only current possibilities and prospective costs are relevant for rational decision-making.

Punishing someone requires hurting them one way or another, which adds to the universal sum-total "stockpile" of harm experienced from this point forward. Therefore, retribution *for its own sake* is irrational and antithetical to our aims.

That's not to say there aren't any good reasons to retaliate. Rather, my point is that punishment can only be justified as a lesser evil. It's simple math. We're trading inflicting a smaller amount of harm now to avoid incurring an anticipated bigger one later on. Thus, in the long term we are still minimizing the universal "stockpile" of harm experienced. Or at least, that's the goal.

Punishment cannot be justified as an end in itself. The belief that someone *deserves* to be punished because they did something wrong is baseless. It cannot be substantiated, merely asserted. The only dubious way I can conceive to keep holding onto this stance is to adopt it on an axiomatic level and utterly forgo the possibility of convincing any skeptic or dissenter. If you can think of any decent alternative, I'd be curious to hear it out, but I must admit I'm not holding my breath.

In summary, "two wrongs don't make a right" is one of the rare platitudes I happen to sanction. It's merely trading one wrong, which already wasn't great, for two, which is even worse. On the flip side, trading one wrong today in the hopes of preventing five expected wrongs tomorrow is a lot more defensible. With that in mind, it's time to address the pitfalls of absolute leniency and pacifism.

## The self-defense corollary

Adopting a naive and simplistic harm reduction stance can end up backfiring. Sometimes the only way to prevent greater expected harm is by inflicting moderate protective harm preemptively.

This is the rationale behind self-defense, which is another ubiquitous precept of our universal moral intuitions. In French, it's known as "legitimate defense", which is a better name in my opinion (if a tad circular), seeing as it doesn't always entail defending oneself.

At first glance it might seem at odds with harm reduction, since it usually involves hurting others. And yet, it's not only tenable but even necessary. Why?

First, without any means to curtail and defend against incoming aggressions, the quantity of harm experienced would only explode under the influence of unchecked threats such as bullies and tyrants. We would end in a world with much more rather than less suffering.

Second, although we like to speak about universal — if not God-given — rights, they're purely products of our minds. In practice they won't transcendently prevent an avalanche, a grizzly bear, or a raider from mauling your ass should they feel so inclined.

The only entities that will partially enforce any entitlement to your well-being are human communities. "Rights" cannot exist without a social contract. Aggressions are a violation of this contract, and they come at a cost. When you fail to upkeep your duties, you lose your claim to the associated privileges, at least in part. The well-being of an aggressor has to be deprioritized relatively to that of their victims.

In short, the "legitimacy" of this type of violence comes from its protective intent and its reactive and mitigating nature, reluctantly compelled by the instigation of an external aggression.

Usually, self-defense is applied in the very short term. A guy lunges at you with a machete, and you shoot him down because that was the only conceivable way you could avoid getting shanked. However, the same general logic, i.e. substituting lesser for greater harm, can extend to broader time scopes.

It is important to note, however, that these adjudications should still only weigh in the present and future stakes. That is to say, the goal isn't to punish perpetrators for past sins, but rather to spare potential future victims.

## When preventive harm is the lesser evil

Here is a list of the main scenarios I can think of where, in my view, a legitimate case for preventive harm can be made:
- to break off the bleed of past harm into the present,
- to prevent recidivism,
- to dismantle a harmful system,
- to prioritize the allocation of limited resources,
- to avoid setting up the wrong incentive structure.

The list isn't sorted by importance. Additionally, the delineation between each of these scenarios is somewhat arbitrary and fuzzy; they will often overlap in practice. However, separating them out will help establish a more comprehensive set of examples. With that said, let's jump into it.

### Heal lingering harm and prevent festering

In this first case, the goal is to prevent the continuation of past harm into the present and future. More specifically, this is mostly about avoiding the negation, gaslighting, and invalidation of the victim's experience. As far as I can tell, not being taken seriously, understood, validated, and respected plays a massive role in the perception of injustice and the instigation of revenge fantasies.

Often, a substantial cause behind trauma isn't merely the injury of the inciting event in itself but also the added insult of the disregard, callousness, and lack of support from your social environment.

Rectifying this type of wrong usually involves a clear acknowledgment of wrongdoing by the offender, or at the very least by the community at large, and some form of accountability or reparation from the former. Depending on what that looks like, this might be considered punishment by some, but in such a case it would only serve as a necessary means to lay the groundwork for healing and moving on, rather than an intrinsic payback entitlement.

Furthermore, I don't think cruel and sadistic means are required. If the offender is earnestly repentant, an official recognition of their responsibility and regret for the harm inflicted combined with a reasonable level of confidence that it will not happen again is oftentimes sufficient.

Ideally, that should be the point of an apology. Namely, to demonstrate the offender has understood why and how they messed up, both for the healing aspect of validation and in the hopes their newly expanded awareness ensures they won't relapse. Not to force them through a little humiliation ritual out of tyrannical pettiness.

Granted, in practice these situations are often messy and more complicated to navigate. Regardless, I think the point still stands, at least in principle. Sadly, whether it's intentional or not, many people muddy the waters by pointing out practical limitations to justify principled cruelty.

Speaking of practical concerns and relapse, let's address the risk of repeat offenses next.

### Prevent recidivism

Another source of legitimate, extrinsic justification for punishment pertains to containment and prevention. If some offenders just can't help themselves from systematically hurting others every chance they get, then it's rational, ethical, and safer for everyone to restrain their ability to do so.

As mentioned earlier, when discussing the arguments for self-defense, when John Doe decides to violate the agency and well-being of his peers, then he partially loses his socially upheld entitlement to his own, to the reasonable extent required to protect said peers.

I think this line of reasoning can sometimes even arguably justify more drastic and extreme prevention measures. To give a concrete example, as far as I'm concerned, Batman should most likely just kill the Joker. Not as punishment or revenge, but to save his future victims.

As repeatedly demonstrated in the DC canon, every time the Joker gets jailed, he always eventually breaks out only to subsequently traumatize, injure, and kill large swaths of people. Attempts at rehabilitation only backfire as he warps his psychologists into new, raving, lunatic minions.

Sure, on a meta level these are just narrative contrivances to keep generating conflict and drama, but that doesn't change the decision-making parameters from within the fiction. Jailing the Joker only amounts to postponing future mass murder and mayhem. So in order for Batman's conscience to stay pure and pristine according to his self-imposed code^[As per comic logic, where battering and crippling average goons for life is A-OK. Tough life for minions. Commit 10% of the crimes, yet receive 150% of your boss' punishment. These guys should unionize.], Gotham's population will have to pay the price in obituaries instead.

With a strong record of systematic recidivism and infinitesimal odds of realistic rehabilitation, containment can become the best harm reduction tradeoff, and failing that, sometimes even elimination.

Obviously, this is a very contentious adjudication, and I can appreciate Batman's wariness about the dizzying risk of this potential slippery slope. There is no hard science for comparing the evaluated likelihood of recidivism versus redemption. It's debatable whether such critical decision-making and power can and should be formally entrusted to anyone.

What if the accused is in fact innocent? Or perhaps they're indeed guilty, but they experienced a sudden and complete change of heart and were never going to hurt anyone again henceforth. In these cases, all moral justification is lost. That's the scary aspect of forecasting-based judgement. It's a Schrödinger coin toss between a grievous miscarriage of justice and an act of self-defense.^[The dystopian movie *Minority Report* explores this moral quandary, although its system operates on a model of punishment for anticipated crimes, rather than harm reduction, and in so doing becomes unjustifiable.]

That's why I wouldn't feel comfortable signing off on an algorithm designed to assess whether the best ethical decision is to just merc any given predator based on probability computations. And yet, on a case-by-case basis, I may sometimes concede the pragmatic rationality in such a choice.

That covers recidivism on the level of individual offenders, but how should we handle the threat of broader, recurrent sources of harm?

### Dismantling harmful systems

Inaction in the face of rising oppression, whether its motivated by complacency or pacifism, can end up causing more harm than the force required to nip it in the bud. Sometimes you have to wage war to preserve peace.

If you want to nurture a world of freedom and compassion, you cannot abstain from confronting rampant tyrannical forces such as the Axis during World War II. Sure, you would avoid bloodying your hands in the short term. However, their victims are granted no such reprieve in the interim, and you allow a festering source of harm to grow its resources and influence unimpeded. Once its power has reached a tipping point, it will have its way over every alternative lifestyle. At that point, any real semblance of peace and justice will have been lost. In the end, the cumulative harm it has generated will have far outweighed whatever protective violence would have been required to stop it from the outset.

Therefore, inflicting some level of harm can be justified when there is no other realistic means to topple oppressive institutions such as totalitarianism or slavery. However, these instances of violence can only be justified when used sparingly as means to stop further harm. They shouldn't be glorified or celebrated. Violence as a vindictive outlet for resentment cannot be justified and should not be tolerated.

With that said, let's move on to the ethical dilemmas that can arise due to scarcity and opportunity cost.

### Prioritizing the allocation of limited resources

Rehabilitation attempts can be expensive, and they're not guaranteed to succeed. They also come at the opportunity cost of potential investments for the benefit of the rest of the community who abide by the social contract. These expenses don't usually represent a significant toll on most developed societies, and in a vast majority of cases denying second chances would end up causing more harm than it prevents.

However, the less affluence a community has access to, the greater the burden of rehabilitation will become and the less leniency it can afford. Not everyone is redeemable, if only due to limited resources, whether it's time, energy, money, manpower, infrastructure, or food. Under scarcity the investments made to reform past offenders come at a steeper cost for the rest of an already struggling community, and the threat of potential recidivism only becomes more dangerous.

Let's take the example of tribal communities, which have been the predominant form of social organization over humanity's lifespan. They typically only number around a hundred members at most, and their food supply relies heavily on hunting and foraging. Most of them lead a nomadic lifestyle and don't have good means of containment. Their cultural norms are usually sufficient to maintain peace and order, especially given the reinforced social pressure of a tight-knit community. However, when faced with a particularly recalcitrant member that repeatedly refuses to abide and keeps harming and threatening everybody else's well-being, the tribe won't have much choice but to eventually resort to exile, if not outright execution.

Fortunately, these types of scenarios tend to be the exception, and in most cases there is no need for such drastic measures. Even so, there are other factors out there that are much trickier to rise above than acute scarcity. One such consideration, which bears relevance in pretty much any context, is how the policies we adopt shape the incentives of our societies.

### Avoid setting up the wrong incentive structure

The way an individual or society reacts to an infraction doesn't merely affect the offender of that specific instance. It also sends a signal to everyone else about what repercussions they should expect. Consequently, you have to keep the bigger picture in mind and consider whether your reaction will bolster or deter future reoccurrences. Being too lenient can foster a culture of impunity over time, where would-be exploiters know they'll easily be able to get away with it.

This overlaps with the concept of [operant conditioning,](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operant_conditioning?wprov=sfla1) which refers to modulating behavior likelihood and frequency through the distribution of rewards and punishments. In an environment with many incentives to be a dipshit and very little not to (aside from basic consideration for your fellow human beings), a decent amount of people are bound to go for it. And through repetition these patterns can get ingrained pretty deeply over time.

One commonplace example is children who grow up to become entitled brats due to excessively permissive and spineless parents who cave to their every whims.

In a more sinister register, the overlapping worlds of tech, business, social media, and politics are rife with many such instances in our current times. Grifters making bank from peddling hate and misinformation. The relentless consolidation of corporations under a handful of ever encroaching, bloated, and noxious monopolies. Corrupt executives and politicians unraveling the fabric of society for any shred of power and wealth they can extract to further their autocratic, egomaniacal ambitions. The list goes on. What motivation do they have to stop, given they barely face any pushback and can easily avoid most repercussions? Unscrupulous exploiters thrive in the Wild West.

With the concerning degradation of the rule of law and the rise of vindictive, authoritarian populism, liberal institutions would do well to drop their festering inequalities and sharpen the defensive edge of their paradox of tolerance if they intend to survive.

The willingness to retaliate swiftly and firmly after a defection is as crucial as forgiving past transgressions and moving on in order to foster sustainable cooperation. I've discussed this topic in greater depth in [my post on the Prisoner's Dilemma and Tit-for-tat](/blog/game-theory-of-cooperation-101/).

Depending on the situation, a striking, and even disproportionate, reckoning can sometimes be necessary to let people know without a shadow of a doubt that if they fuck around, they'll find out. Not out of spite but to send a clear message that such behavior will not be tolerated.

## Conclusion

So, to sum things up, the central priority in any of these ethical dilemmas should always be to minimize harm and suffering in the present and in the future. Justice isn't meting out punishments. It's protecting, restoring, and nurturing human flourishing.

At times it's better to smite decisively from the onset if it can stem a recurrent stream of harm that would quickly overshadow the measures required to prevent it by compounding over time. But that should never be the gleeful end. It can only be justified as an unfortunate yet necessary means.

Do not conflate circumstantial case with principle, precedent with reason, nor anger and spite with justice.

I hope this post can serve as my modest yet constructive contribution to waking up the zeitgeist from its cruel torpor and moving along the tracks of moral progress by discarding the backward, heartless, and mindless notion of punitive justice in the dusty bin of our sordid history along with its brethren ("an eye for an eye", "guilty until proven innocent", etc.) and in so doing prevent much needless suffering thereafter.

Plus, it gets a bit lonely and scary to feel surrounded by hordes of blood-thirsty, vindictive maniacs sometimes.
